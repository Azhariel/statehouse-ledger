The [[governor of Pennsylvania]] is the [[head of government]] of the [[Commonwealth (U.S. state)|Commonwealth]] of [[Pennsylvania]], as well as [[commander-in-chief]] of the state's [[Pennsylvania National Guard|national guard]].<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 7</ref>

The [[Governor (United States)|governor]] has a duty to enforce state laws and the power to approve or [[veto]] [[Bill (law)|bills]] passed by the [[Pennsylvania General Assembly]],<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 15</ref> as well as to convene the legislature.<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 12</ref> The governor may grant pardons except in cases of [[Impeachment in the United States|impeachment]], but only when recommended by the Board of Pardons.<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 9</ref> 

There have been seven presidents and 48 governors of Pennsylvania, with two governors ([[Robert E. Pattison]] and [[Gifford Pinchot]]) serving non-consecutive terms, totaling 55 terms in both offices. The longest term was that of the first governor, [[Thomas Mifflin]], who served three full terms as governor in addition to two years as [[President of the Continental Congress]]. The shortest term belonged to [[John C. Bell Jr.]], who served only 19 days as acting governor after his predecessor, [[Edward Martin (Pennsylvania politician)|Edward Martin]], resigned.

The current governor is Democrat [[Josh Shapiro]], who took office on January 17, 2023.

==Governors==
{{For|the period before independence|List of colonial governors of Pennsylvania}}
Pennsylvania was one of the original [[Thirteen Colonies]] and was admitted as a state on December 12, 1787.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Ratification of the Constitution by the State of Pennsylvania; December 12, 1787 |url=http://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/ratpa.asp |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151105061228/http://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/ratpa.asp |archive-date=November 5, 2015 |access-date=October 31, 2015 |publisher=The Avalon Project at Yale Law School}}</ref> Before it declared its independence, Pennsylvania was a [[Province of Pennsylvania|colony]] of the [[Kingdom of Great Britain]].

===Presidents of the Supreme Executive Council===
The [[Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776]] created the [[Supreme Executive Council of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania|Supreme Executive Council]] as the state's executive branch, with a president as its head.<ref>1776 Constitution § 3</ref> The president was chosen annually by the council, though with no specific term dates.<ref>1776 Constitution § 19</ref>

The constitution created the position of "vice-president", though no provision was made if the office of the president became vacant, which occurred four times later. Contemporary sources continue to label the chief executive in such times as the vice-president, without any notion of succeeding in the presidency. One acting president, [[George Bryan]], was subsequently recognized later as a full-fledged governor, due to his acting as president for over six months.

===Governors of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania===
[[File:Five PA-Governors.jpg|thumb|right|Five governors of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania who  have served since 1995, (left to right): [[Mark Schweiker]], [[Tom Ridge]], [[Tom Wolf]], [[Tom Corbett]] and [[Ed Rendell]], pose in front of the east facade of the [[Pennsylvania State Capitol]] in [[Harrisburg, Pennsylvania|Harrisburg]] at Wolf's January 2015 gubernatorial inauguration]]

The 1790 constitution abolished the council and replaced the president with a governor,<ref>1790 Constitution article II, § 1</ref> and established a three-year term for governor commencing on the third Tuesday of the December following the election, with governors not allowed to serve more than nine out of any twelve years.<ref>1790 Constitution article IV, § 3</ref> The 1838 constitution moved the start of the term to the third Tuesday of the January following the election, and allowed governors to only serve six out of any nine years.<ref>1838 Constitution article II, § 3</ref> The 1874 constitution lengthened the term to four years, and prohibited governors from succeeding themselves.<ref>1874 Constitution article IV, § 3</ref> The current constitution of 1968 changed this to allow governors to serve two consecutive terms, with no lifetime limit.<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 3</ref>

Under the 1968 constitution, [[Milton Shapp]] was the first governor to serve two terms, and [[Tom Corbett]] was the first incumbent governor to lose a reelection bid.

If the office of governor becomes vacant through death, resignation, or conviction on impeachment, the [[Lieutenant Governor of Pennsylvania|lieutenant governor]] becomes governor for the remainder of the term; if the office is only temporarily vacant due to disability of the governor, the lieutenant governor only acts out the duties of governor.<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 13</ref> Should both offices be vacant, the [[president pro tempore|president ''pro tempore'']] of the state senate becomes governor.<ref>PA Constitution article IV, § 14</ref> The position of a lieutenant  governor was created in the 1874 constitution; prior to then, the speaker of the senate would act as governor in cases of vacancy. Originally, the lieutenant governor could only act as governor; it was not until the [[Pennsylvania Constitution|1968 constitution]] that the lieutenant governor could actually become the sitting governor in that fashion. The office of governor has been vacant for an extended period once before, a 17-day gap in 1848 between the resignation of the previous governor and the swearing in of his acting successor. Governors and lieutenant governors are elected on the same [[political party]] [[ticket (election)|ticket]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Executive Branch of the Several States |url=http://www.thegreenpapers.com/slg/executive.phtml |access-date=April 30, 2008 |publisher=The Green Papers}}</ref>
