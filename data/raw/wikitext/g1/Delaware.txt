The '''Governor of Delaware''' (known as the '''President of Delaware''' from 1776 to 1792) is the [[head of government]] of [[Delaware]] and the commander-in-chief of the [[U.S. state|state's]] [[Delaware National Guard|military forces]]. The [[Governor (United States)|governor]] has a duty to enforce state laws, and the power to either approve or [[veto]] bills passed by the [[Delaware General Assembly|Delaware Legislature]], to convene the legislature,{{sfn|Del. Const.|loc=art. III}} and to grant [[pardon]]s, except in cases of impeachment, and only with the recommendation of the Board of Pardons.{{sfn|Del. Const.|loc=[https://50constitutions.org/de/constitution/section-id-56444 art. VII, § 1]}}

There have been 71 people who have served as governor, over 74 distinct terms. Three ([[Joseph Haslet]], [[Charles Polk Jr.]] and [[Elbert N. Carvel]]) served non-consecutive terms. Additionally, [[Henry Molleston]] was elected, but died before he could take office. Only four governors have been elected to two consecutive terms, with the longest-serving being [[Ruth Ann Minner]], who was elected twice after succeeding to the office, serving a total of just over eight years. The shortest term is that of [[Bethany Hall-Long]], who served 15 days following her predecessor's resignation; [[Dale E. Wolf]] served 18 days and [[David P. Buckson]] served 19 days under similar circumstances. The current governor is [[Delaware Democratic Party|Democrat]] [[Matt Meyer]], who took office on January 21, 2025.

==Governors==
Before 1776, Delaware was a [[Delaware Colony|colony]] of the [[Kingdom of Great Britain]], administered by [[List of colonial governors of Pennsylvania|colonial governors]] in [[Pennsylvania]] as the "Lower Counties on Delaware".

In 1776, soon after Delaware and the rest of the [[Thirteen Colonies]] declared independence from Britain, the state adopted its [[Delaware Constitution of 1776|first state constitution]]. It created the office of President of Delaware, a chief executive to be chosen by the legislature to serve a term of three years, who would be ineligible to be president again until three years had passed after leaving office.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=563|loc=1776 art. 7}}

The office of President was renamed Governor by the [[Delaware Constitution of 1792|constitution of 1792]],{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=572|loc=1792 art. III, § 1}} which set the commencement date of the term to the third Tuesday in the January following an election, and limited governors to serving only three out of any six years.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=573|loc=1792 art. III, § 3}} The term was lengthened to four years by the [[Delaware Constitution of 1831|1831 constitution]], but governors were limited to a single term.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=587|loc=1831 art. III, § 3}} The [[Constitution of Delaware|current constitution of 1897]] allows governors to serve two terms.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=610|loc=1897 art. III, § 5}}

The 1776 constitution stated that if the presidency were vacant, the speaker of the legislative council would be a vice-president.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=563|loc=1776 art. 7}} The 1792 constitution had the speaker of the senate exercising the office when it is vacant,{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|pp=573–574|loc=1792 art. III, § 14}} and the 1897 constitution created the office of lieutenant governor,{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=613|loc=1897 art. III, § 19}} upon whom the office devolves in case of vacancy.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=613|loc=1897 art. III, § 20}} The offices of governor and lieutenant governor are elected at the same time but not on the same [[ticket (election)|ticket]].
