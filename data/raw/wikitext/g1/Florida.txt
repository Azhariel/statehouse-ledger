The [[governor of Florida]] is the [[head of government]] of the [[U.S. state]] of [[Florida]]. The [[Governor (United States)|governor]] is the head of the [[Government of Florida#Executive branch|executive branch]] of the [[government of Florida]] and is the [[commander-in-chief]] of the [[Florida National Guard]] and [[Florida State Guard]].

The current officeholder is [[Ron DeSantis]], a member of the [[Republican Party of Florida|Republican Party]] who took office on January 8, 2019.

===State of Florida===
The State of Florida was [[admission to the Union|admitted to the Union]] on March 3, 1845. It seceded from the [[Union (American Civil War)|Union]] on January 10, 1861,<ref>{{Cite web |title=Secession Ordinances of 13 Confederate States |url=http://www.digitalhistory.uh.edu/disp_textbook.cfm?smtID=3&psid=3953 |url-status=live |year=2018 |last1=Mintz |first1=S. |last2=McNeil |first2=S. |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150905091739/http://www.digitalhistory.uh.edu/disp_textbook.cfm?smtID=3&psid=3953 |archive-date=September 5, 2015 |access-date=October 1, 2026 |website=Digital History}}</ref> and joined the [[Confederate States of America]] on February 8, 1861,<ref>{{cite book |url=https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=dul1.ark:/13960/t2t44fh2r&seq=23 |page=1 |title=The Statutes at Large of the Provisional Government of the Confederate States of America |editor-last=Matthews |editor-first=James M. |year=1864 |location=Richmond, Virginia |publisher=R.M. Smith |via=[[HathiTrust]]}}</ref> as a founding member. Following the end of the [[American Civil War]], it was part of the [[Third Military District]].<ref>{{Cite journal |last=Cox |first=Merlin |date=January 1968 |title=Military Reconstruction in Florida |journal=Florida Historical Quarterly |volume=46 |issue=3 |page=219}}</ref> Florida was readmitted to the Union on June 25, 1868.{{efn|The readmission was contingent on Florida ratifying the [[Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution]];<ref>{{usstat|15|73}}</ref> they were proclaimed on July 11, 1868, to have done so before the readmission act's passage.<ref>{{usstat|15|703}}</ref>}}

The [[Constitution of Florida|Florida Constitution]] of 1838 provided that a governor be elected every 4 years, who was not allowed to serve consecutive terms.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=666|loc=1838 art. III, § 2}} The secessionist constitution of 1861 would have reduced this to two years and removed the term limit,<ref>{{cite book |url=https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/pt?id=dul1.ark:/13960/t8w960178&seq=7 |title=Constitution of Florida of 1861 |page=6 |via=[[HathiTrust]]}}</ref> but the state fell to the Union before the first election under that constitution. The 1868 constitution maintained the four-year term,{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=710|loc=1868 art. VI, § 2}} though without the earlier term limit, which was reintroduced in the 1885 constitution.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=739|loc=1885 art. IV, § 2}} The current constitution of 1968 states that should the governor serve, or would have served had he not resigned, more than six years in two consecutive terms, he cannot be elected to the succeeding term.{{sfn|Fla. Const.|loc=[https://50constitutions.org/fl/constitution/section-id-64838 art. IV, § 5]}} The start of a term was set in 1885 at the first Tuesday after the first Monday in the January following the election,{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=739|loc=1885 art. IV, § 2}} where it has remained.{{sfn|Fla. Const.|loc=[https://50constitutions.org/fl/constitution/section-id-64838 art. IV, § 5]}}

Originally, the president of the state senate acted as governor should that office be vacant.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=668|loc=1838 art. III, § 18}} The 1868 constitution created the office of [[Lieutenant Governor of Florida|lieutenant governor]],{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=712|loc=1868 art. VI, § 15}} who would similarly act as governor. This office was abolished in 1885, with the president of the senate again taking on that duty.{{sfn|Thorpe|1909|p=741|loc=1885 art. IV, § 19}} The 1968 constitution recreated the office of lieutenant governor, who now becomes governor in the absence of the governor.{{sfn|Fla. Const.|loc=[https://50constitutions.org/fl/constitution/section-id-64827 art. IV, § 3]}} The governor and lieutenant governor are elected on the same [[ticket (election)|ticket]].{{sfn|Fla. Const.|loc=[https://50constitutions.org/fl/constitution/section-id-64838 art. IV, § 5]}}

Florida was a strongly [[Florida Democratic Party|Democratic]] state before the Civil War, electing only one candidate from the [[Whig Party (United States)|Whig Party]]. It elected three [[Republican Party of Florida|Republican]] governors following Reconstruction, but after the Democratic Party re-established control, 90 years passed before voters chose another Republican. Since 1998, it has been a strongly Republican state.

==Acting governor==
Florida has had a number of people serve as acting governor. The state's first three constitutions provided that the succession in office became operative whenever the governor was out of the state. Thus, in 1853 when Governor Thomas Brown attended an event in Boston—the Senate president who would normally succeed the governor at the time was also out of state. Therefore, the speaker of the Florida House of Representatives, A.K. Allison, became acting governor on September 16, 1853. He served for 17 days.<ref>Allen Morris & Joan Perry Morris, "The Florida Handbook" 2007-2008.</ref>

Article IV Section 3 (b) of the Florida Constitution now calls for the lieutenant governor to "act as Governor" during the governor's physical or mental incapacity. This provision has been invoked one time.  On June 18, 2008, Governor Charlie Crist filed a proclamation with the secretary of state transferring power of governor to Lt. Governor Jeff Kottkamp pursuant to the constitutional provision while he underwent knee surgery.<ref>{{Cite news |title=Gov. Crist Has Surgery on Knee |newspaper=[[The Ledger]] |date=June 19, 2008 |url=https://www.newspapers.com/article/the-ledger-gov-crist-has-surgery-on-kne/208645693/ |access-date=October 19, 2026 |page=B2 |via=Newspapers.com}}</ref>
