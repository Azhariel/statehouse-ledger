About the data

How the count works

Each year goes to the party in charge of a state on July 1, which is whoever held office for most of that year. The number in each state is the running lead: years for the leading party minus years for the other. It shows in the leading party's color, or in white when the party in charge right now is also the one ahead.

The two scoring rules

  • Governor: the year goes to the governor's party.
  • 2 of 3: the year goes to whichever party holds at least two of the governorship, the state Senate and the state House. Otherwise the state is split and the year counts for nobody. Tied or coalition-run chambers count for nobody. Where the legislature was nonpartisan (Nebraska since 1937, Minnesota from 1913 to 1973) or has no recorded data, the governor decides.

Party labels are not ideology

The count follows party names, not positions. The Democrats who ran the South for about a century after Reconstruction were mostly conservative, and the region realigned toward the Republicans between roughly the 1960s and the 1990s. That is why Texas and Georgia carry large blue numbers when counted from 1856. Pick a later start year (or your birth year) to see the modern picture.

Judgment calls

  • Civil War "Union" and "National Union" governors and legislators count as Republican, except in Kentucky, where the Unionists were ex-Whigs and Union Democrats who opposed the Republicans.
  • Reconstruction-era "Conservative" parties in the South count as Democratic, since they were the Democratic Party under another name.
  • North Dakota's Nonpartisan League and Independent Voters Association count as Republican, since both ran inside the state GOP.
  • Andrew Johnson is shown as "other": he was a Democrat elected on Lincoln's National Union ticket.
  • Where two rival governments claimed a state (Civil War and Reconstruction), the one holding the state capital counts.
  • Presidential provisional governors, military rule and vacancies count for no party. Whigs, Know Nothings, Populists and other third parties count for neither side.

Known gaps

Wikipedia's legislature tables leave some years blank, mostly in the 19th century South (Mississippi, Georgia, Arkansas, Kentucky, Alabama) and in Confederate states during the Civil War. In those years the "2 of 3" score uses what is known, and the state's pop-up says "No data" for the missing chamber.
